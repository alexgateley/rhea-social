One $1 sandwich, worked out. Example numbers, ours, not Burger King's or any franchisee's.

Food and paper $0.50, crew time $0.30: prime cost $0.80, or 80% of the price. Many quick-service operators aim for 55 to 60% across the menu (Restaurant365). Royalty and ad fund are a percent of sales, so they come off the top: $0.08 here, leaving $0.12 for rent, utilities, the manager and the loan. This example store needs $0.23 an item for those, so each sandwich loses $0.11.

The same sandwich at $2.09 leaves $1.12, more than nine times as much. Add fries and a drink and the check's prime cost falls to about 44%. The whole check has to pay, not one item.

When the court dismissed the franchisees' case in November 2010, it found they had "improperly focused on losses from a single product sold below cost" rather than on their overall business, as summarized by the law firm Lathrop GPM. The judge also wrote, as quoted by Consumerist, "Nothing about this action suggests bad faith."

Burger King's 2009 annual report gave U.S. royalties of 3.5% to 4.5% of gross sales (3.9% on average) and an advertising contribution of typically 4%.
